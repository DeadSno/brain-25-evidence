First, three questions

1. How is a supplement different from a drug?

A supplement complements nutrition, it does not replace treatment. Supplements are not registered as drugs, but meta-analyses exist — those are what we count.

2. Why do I need it?

Three honest reasons: a deficiency (vitamin D in winter), a goal (creatine for training), evidence-based prevention (omega-3).

3. How to choose?

Verdict and evidence grade → dosage from the studies → drug conflicts → and only then the price.

Why you can trust this

  • The verdict is set by hand, not by a formula. A machine computes the science index, but the final “USEFUL / Possible / Not confirmed” we assign ourselves — after reading the two strongest meta-analyses.
  • Every link can be opened and checked. All 130 of 130 supplements carry a PMID and a meta-analysis with a PubMed link on the card.
  • Drug interactions are sorted by severity. 199 pairs: 9 critical, 24 high, 74 moderate, 92 low — each one describes what happens when combined.
  • The data sits right there; you can recompute it. 15 JSON files in the docs/ folder — take them and check for yourself.
  • Three answers, not two. If there are few meta-analyses, it stays “Possible” — it never turns into “USEFUL”. Contested effects are flagged right on the card.

📐 What is the “science index”?

Science index = RCTs + 5 × meta-analyses in PubMed for the query “(supplement) AND (outcome)”.

The index measures the amount of science, not the effect size. Verdicts are assigned manually from the top-2 meta-analyses.

🎯 What is “effect size” (Hedges' g)?

Hedges' g is the standard metric of how much a supplement really helps compared with placebo.

How to read it:

  • 0 — no effect (like placebo)
  • ±0.2 — small effect (noticeable but modest)
  • ±0.5 — medium effect (clinically meaningful)
  • ±0.8+ — large effect (obvious to anyone)
  • Negative — does not mean “bad”. It depends on what was measured: e.g. for Magnesium g = −0.67 on the anxiety scale is a decrease in anxiety, which is good

We take g from the first relevant meta-analysis for each supplement. One supplement can have many g values for different outcomes (sleep, strength, memory) — we show one example, not the “main effect”.

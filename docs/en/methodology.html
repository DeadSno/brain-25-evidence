Methodology

What is audit v

The audit is a full manual verification of 130 supplements using an adapted PRISMA methodology (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses). We implemented 6 key stages: identification → screening → deduplication → abstract reading → source appraisal → synthesis.

What we do not do: we do not read full texts (only the top-15 abstracts), we do not run double-blind screening, we do not register the protocol in PROSPERO, and we do not formally assess the risk of bias. This is a deliberate simplification to cover 130 supplements with one person. Every supplement went through:

  1. Search PubMed — systematic reviews and meta-analyses (MA/SR)
  2. Screening — filtering by relevance
  3. Deduplication — removing duplicates and updates
  4. Reading the top-15 abstracts — extracting effect data
  5. Assigning grade A–D — based on the strength of evidence

Result: 130 audit cards in data/processed/audit/, 14 audit commits, tags v3.0-audit-top20 and v3.0-audit-complete.

PRISMA funnel

For every supplement we run the standard systematic review funnel:

Found: 100-500 articles (PubMed: MA + SR)
    ↓
Screening: 20-100 relevant (title filter)
    ↓
Unique: 10-90 (dedupe by authors/years)
    ↓
Read: top-15 (sort: Cochrane → year → number of RCTs)
    ↓
Audit card: grade + verdict + effect_outcome + caution

Example: Creatine — 132 found, 36 screened, 35 unique, 15 read → grade A, verdict “works”.

Grades A–D

Grades are assigned manually after reading the abstracts, based on the strength of evidence:

  • A — works very well: multiple Cochrane reviews, large RCTs, strong effect (Hedges' g ≥ 0.4). Examples: Vitamin D, Creatine, Iron, Caffeine, B9, Whey protein.
  • B — works: systematic reviews exist, the effect is confirmed, but there is less data or the effect is moderate. Examples: Omega-3, Zinc, Magnesium, Melatonin, CoQ10.
  • C — depends on context: data exist, but the effect is small, contradictory or present only under specific conditions. Examples: Resveratrol, NMN, Chromium, Valerian.
  • D — not confirmed: no systematic reviews exist, or they show no effect. Examples: Biotin, Yohimbine, Tribulus, Chlorophyll.

Important: grades are assigned by a human, not by a formula. This allows us to account for nuances (RCT quality, clinical relevance, side effects).

Verdicts

Every supplement gets one of three verdicts:

  • works — the effect is confirmed by systematic reviews
  • depends on context — the effect exists but is limited to specific conditions (dose, population, duration)
  • not proven — there are no systematic data, or they show no effect

What an audit card contains

Each of the 130 cards (data/processed/audit/*.md) contains:

  • PRISMA funnel: found → screened → unique → read
  • JSON block: id, category, verdict, grade, effect_outcome, caution, key_sources (PMID), updated
  • Verdicts by context: which grade for which condition (e.g. sleep vs. anxiety)
  • Key sources: PMIDs of the top-3 systematic reviews

Grade distribution (130 supplements)

  • A (works very well): 6 supplements (4.6%)
  • B (works): 22 supplements (16.9%)
  • C (depends on context): 52 supplements (40.0%)
  • D (not confirmed): 50 supplements (38.5%)

This is an honest distribution: not “everything works”, but the real picture of the evidence.

Conflicts of interest (COI)

A conflict of interest (COI) is a link between a study author and anyone who could benefit from the result: sponsorship by a manufacturer, collaboration with a pharmaceutical company, a position in the industry. It is not a verdict on the quality of the work, but it is a factor the reader must know.

We review COI in all 6310 full texts we read during the audit and check them against the disclosure block in the paper itself:

  • Conflict declared: 467 papers — 8,5% of those that had any disclosure at all
  • No conflict declared: 4,349 papers
  • Unclear: 674 papers — the wording does not allow a clear judgement
  • Not disclosed: 820 papers

That is, in 5490 cases out of 6,310 (87%) the authors declared something, and only 8.5% of those who declared anything mentioned an industry link.

Separately we count funding markers in the papers themselves: sponsorship is mentioned in 3500 papers (55.5%), mentions of pharmaceutical companies — in 282 (4.5%). These counters are independent of COI: a paper can declare no conflict while describing sponsorship — exactly such discrepancies are what we flag.

Important: the presence of a COI does not mean the study conclusions are wrong — systematic reviews with conflicts of interest are counted on par with the rest. We show the fact and leave the trust decision to the reader.
